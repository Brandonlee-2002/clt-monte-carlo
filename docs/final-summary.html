Final Summary

What the simulations say about sample size, shape, and assumptions

1 Research question

The project asks whether there is a universal sample size at which a sampling distribution of a mean can be called approximately normal. I tested that idea with the same Monte Carlo design across populations with different shapes and assumption structures.

2 Experimental design

For each population and each candidate (n), I generated B = 10000 sample means. The candidate sizes were 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 300, 500. The student seed is set immediately before every experiment. This makes each row reproducible and allows a second seed to be used as a sensitivity check.

The operational definition of “approximately normal” requires all four of the following:

  • absolute skewness ≤ 0.20;
  • absolute excess kurtosis ≤ 0.30;
  • normal Q-Q correlation ≥ 0.995;
  • standardized normal Q-Q RMSE ≤ 0.08.

The selected (n) is the first candidate that passes all four criteria for three consecutive investigated sizes. This stability requirement prevents a single Monte Carlo fluctuation from determining the answer. Histograms, theoretical standard deviations, and the population assumptions are reviewed alongside the numerical screen.

4 Answer to the research question

The answer is ambiguous because the question asks for a smallest value of (n), but the phrase “reasonably defensible as approximately normal” depends on several choices. It is not a property that switches on at one universal sample size.

First, the answer depends on the population being averaged. In this project, the current run uses seed 6599 and B = 10000 repetitions. The first stable passes were:

These results are evidence against treating (n=30) as a universal rule. For example, the Poisson population did pass at (n=30), with skewness about 0.142, excess kurtosis about -0.007, Q-Q correlation about 0.998, and Q-Q RMSE about 0.064. By contrast, the Exponential population still had skewness about 0.398 at (n=30), so it failed the same standard; it first passed at (n=100), where skewness was about 0.189. The rare-success binomial population passed at (n=50), not at the conventional rule-of-thumb value of 30.

Second, the answer depends on what “approximately normal” means. I made the judgment reproducible by requiring all four shape criteria—absolute skewness at most 0.20, absolute excess kurtosis at most 0.30, Q-Q correlation at least 0.995, and standardized Q-Q RMSE at most 0.08. I also required three consecutive passing candidate sizes. A researcher using a looser skewness threshold, a different Q-Q tolerance, or only visual inspection could select a smaller value. That does not necessarily mean one analysis is wrong; it means the analyses answer slightly different operational questions.

Third, “smallest” is relative to the sample sizes investigated. The simulation tested (n=2,5,10,15,20,30,40,50,75,100,150,200,300,500). Thus, for example, the reported (n=100) for the exponential population means the first stable passing value on this grid. It does not prove that every value between 75 and 100 fails. A finer search could refine the boundary.

Fourth, Monte Carlo results are estimates of the sampling distributions, not perfect observations of them. Changing the random seed changes the exact skewness, kurtosis, and Q-Q measurements. That is why the project records the seed, uses 10,000 repetitions, and requires consecutive passes. The broad conclusions are more meaningful than the last decimal place.

Finally, the usual CLT assumptions matter. The Cauchy sample means did not produce a stable normal approximation through (n=500); at (n=500), the simulated skewness was about 87.46, excess kurtosis about 8213.51, and Q-Q correlation only about 0.093. The dependent process also failed at (n=500), with skewness about 0.282 even though its Q-Q correlation was about 0.998. A large nominal sample size does not automatically create a large amount of independent information.

Therefore, the most professional answer is: there is no single universal smallest sample size. For a specified population, diagnostic standard, simulation design, and purpose, the smallest defensible value is the first sample size whose evidence meets the stated standard. In this project, that value ranges from (n=2) to (n=100) for the finite-variance independent populations, while the dependent and Cauchy cases do not meet the standard within the investigated range.

5 Population shape

Symmetry is associated with faster convergence in these experiments. The exponential, rare-success binomial, and two-component mixture cases retain right-skew or tail behavior longer. The Poisson case is also skewed, but its skewness is less extreme than the rare-success binomial case. These differences show why the population distribution matters when choosing a practical sample size.

6 Assumptions and limitations

The Cauchy experiment is the exception to the finite-variance pattern: no stable sample-mean normality was found through (n=500), which is consistent with its lack of a finite mean and variance. The dependent process shows why nominal sample size can overstate the amount of independent information. The non-identical Bernoulli process shows that changing probabilities affect the variance calculation even when a bell-shaped average still develops.

The dependent machine-failure and mixture analyses use explicit models because the handout labels those population specifications as “coming soon.” If the instructor supplies official generators, those generator functions should be replaced while keeping the documented design and diagnostic rule unchanged.

The conclusion is considered reproducible when the broad decision—such as “passes early,” “requires a large (n),” or “no stable (n) in the range”—does not change. The Cauchy case is expected to be the most seed-sensitive because its tails do not provide stable ordinary moments.

8 Overall conclusion

The Central Limit Theorem describes an asymptotic tendency, not a universal sample-size switch. A responsible normal approximation requires attention to population shape, dependence, identical-distribution assumptions, tail behavior, and the purpose of the approximation. Monte Carlo simulation makes those differences visible and provides evidence for a population-specific judgment.
